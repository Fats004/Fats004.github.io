HEX-01 Undergraduate thesis · 2026 Hardware validated

HexaPod

Author — mechanical design, kinematics, firmware, control pipeline

An eighteen-degree-of-freedom walking robot built end to end as my thesis project. Each of the six legs has three joints, so every step is a coordinated solution of the inverse kinematics for three limbs at once while the other three hold the body up. A hexapod is statically stable — it never has to catch itself — which makes it the right platform for slow, deliberate work on ground a wheeled robot cannot cross.

The control path runs from a MATLAB model down to the joints: analytic inverse kinematics generate the foot trajectories, an ESP32 carries them over Wi-Fi as TCP/JSON packets, and an OpenCM9.04 board translates them into position commands for the servo chain. Getting that path stable meant resolving a core conflict in the Bluepad32 stack, remapping servo IDs, fabricating a custom UART board to make the two controllers talk cleanly, and tuning gait timing until the body stopped fighting its own legs.

The simulation and the hardware share one gait model, so a change to the tripod timing in MATLAB is the same change the robot walks with. Current work extends that model from straight-line walking to in-place rotation.

QUETZAL-2 attitude determination & control

Subsystem lead — ADCS design, analysis and flight documentation

QUETZAL-2 is Guatemala's second nanosatellite. I lead the subsystem that answers two questions in orbit: which way is the satellite pointing, and what keeps it from tumbling. The answer to the second is entirely passive — a permanent NdFeB magnet aligns the body with Earth's field, and four hysteresis rods bleed off rotational energy as magnetic losses. No reaction wheels, no magnetorquers, nothing that can fail closed.

Determination is the active half. A magnetometer, an inertial unit and photodiode sun sensors share a single 400 kHz I²C bus; TRIAD gives the first attitude estimate from the field and sun vectors, and a multiplicative extended Kalman filter written in C++ carries it forward between measurements. Because the control magnet sits inside the same spacecraft as the magnetometer, I ran a magnetic cleanliness analysis across three measurement campaigns to separate the field the satellite makes from the field it is trying to measure — including parasitic interference from the deorbit module's own magnets.

The subsystem is documented the way flight hardware has to be: controlled electronics and mechanical design documents under revision, a bill of materials with every part classified as magnetic or not, and as-built geometry that has already forced one design revision when a zero-clearance crossing turned up in the layout.

Electrodynamic tape tether for deorbit

Physics model, configuration trade study, materials validation

A deorbit module that removes a small satellite from orbit using nothing but the environment it flies through. A bare aluminium tape deployed from the spacecraft collects electrons from the ionospheric plasma along its length; the resulting current, crossed with Earth's magnetic field, produces a Lorentz force that opposes the orbital velocity. No propellant, no thruster, no stored energy — the drag comes from the plasma and the field.

I rebuilt the physics model after finding three errors in the earlier design that each broke the concept in a different way: a tape thickness that no supplier could actually manufacture, an overestimate of how strongly the tether couples to the geomagnetic field, and an incorrect formulation of orbital-motion-limited current collection. The corrected model settles on a 200 m × 15 mm × 25 µm Al-1350 tape as the primary configuration, backed by a trade study of five candidate materials scored against flight heritage, conductivity and handling.

The honest conclusion of that work is a risk, not a result: the deployment mechanism, at roughly 400–500 g, is heavy enough that it may push the minimum viable host spacecraft from 2U to 3U. That constraint matters more to the mission than any refinement of the tape itself.

AUT-CB Industrial Automation · UVG Built & commissioned

Conveyor cell for weight-based sorting

PLC programming, control panel wiring, HMI, mechanical build

A working conveyor that weighs each package as it arrives and routes it to the correct output. The logic runs on a Siemens PLC programmed in STL under SIMATIC Manager — statement list rather than ladder, which forced me to think about the scan cycle explicitly instead of leaning on graphical shorthand.

I wired the control panel to industrial practice, built an HMI so an operator can set thresholds and watch counts without touching the program, and fabricated the mechanics from 3D-printed parts on an MDF frame with a modular plastic belt. Sensors detect package arrival and position, the drive motor indexes the belt, and the sorting decision happens between one part leaving the scale and the next one reaching it.

It is a teaching-scale machine, but the whole chain is real: sensor to input card, logic to output card, output to actuator, and an interface a person who did not write the code can actually operate.

SUM-01

Sumo robot

A 20 × 20 cm combat robot under 800 g, driven over Bluetooth from an Xbox One controller through an ESP32. I drew the schematic and laid out the PCB in Altium Designer, and modelled the chassis in Fusion 360 — parts came out of laser cutting, PLA printing and resin printing depending on what each one had to survive.

ANI-01

Animatronic eyes

A 3D-printed eye mechanism moved by six servos, driven in C from an Arduino Nano linked over UART to an Adafruit board. The interesting part was the limits: every servo had to be mapped to the range its linkage could actually reach, because a command past the mechanical stop destroys the joint.

R2D-01

R2-D2 replica

A working R2-D2 with no microcontroller and no firmware anywhere in it — the behaviour is wired directly into the circuit. Two DC motors drive the wheels and a servo turns the dome, all sequenced by discrete components.

VIS-01

Vision-based line following

Image-based visual servoing on a Pololu 3Pi with an onboard camera. One region of interest yields one centroid feature, and a proportional law on that error steers the robot at constant velocity — with a first-order filter on the turn rate so the motors are never commanded abruptly. Built in MATLAB and Webots so it ports straight to the physical robot.

Also: LoRa sensor-network node development and PCB design for distributed long-range sensing.
